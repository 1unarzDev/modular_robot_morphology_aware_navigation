\title{Where Can a Modular Robot Change Shape?\\Joint Route, Morphology, and Site Selection\\under Transition Feasibility and Sensing Constraints}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Rishan Checker}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Independent Researcher}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A self-reconfigurable mobile robot must decide not only \emph{which} body to use on each part of a route but \emph{where} it can physically change shape. Both bodies can fit at a site while the module motions that connect them, or the maneuver that verifies the new body, collide; and docking can fail where the relative-pose estimate is poor. We study a hybrid planner over pose, heading, and morphology that admits a reconfiguration edge only after sweeping every relocating module and the verification maneuver through the 3D environment and predicting that each moved module will be observed well enough to dock. On a simulated six-pod robot (ROS~2, Gazebo, Nav2) that detaches, relocates, and re-docks its pods, we compare it with route-first, geometry-only, and feasibility-only rungs of an ablation ladder that share one execution stack, in a paired, layout-clustered study of 60 generated layouts and 720 missions whose outcomes, analysis, smallest effect of interest (0.18 in completion probability), and sample size were fixed before data. \pending{confirmatory data --- primary contrasts with 95\% layout-bootstrap intervals and Holm-adjusted sign-flip $p$ values; secondary deadline-penalized time.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modular robots that reconfigure during a task trade a fixed body for a choice of bodies; for a mobile robot the most direct use is passage, a compact body for open space and a narrower one for a doorway. Choosing the morphology along a route is established: planners select among shapes during coverage~\cite{le2018htetro,cheng2019htetro,cheng2020htetro}, variable bases couple driving and footprint change through model-predictive control~\cite{pankert2022base}, and modular systems choose configurations from perceived requirements~\cite{daudelin2018perception,jing2016smores} or reconfigure autonomously with onboard sensing~\cite{tu2026freesn}. We claim neither the first morphology-aware navigation stack nor the first autonomous reconfiguration system.

What these layers leave open is the transformation as a spatially located physical event. Task planners treat a morphology change as a switch between two admissible configurations, and reconfiguration planners take the target and its location as given~\cite{yoshida2002reconfig,liu2019distributed}. Yet at a given site both bodies may be collision-free while a relocating module's path is not; the relocation may be clear while the maneuver that verifies the new body is not; and the docking that closes the transition may need a pose estimate the site cannot supply. Where the robot can change shape then depends on the route, and the route on where it can change shape (Fig.~\ref{fig:sitemap}).

\textbf{Research question.} Does joint route and morphology selection, conditioned on full 3D transition feasibility and on sensing uncertainty at the transition site, improve mission completion under a fixed deadline relative to route-first adaptation, geometry-only coupled planning, and feasibility-only coupled planning, when all four share mechanics, sensing, maps, controllers, and disturbances?

\textbf{Contribution.} A bounded hypothesis under test, not a claim that no prior system does this: (1) a hybrid planner over $(x, y, \theta, m)$ whose reconfiguration edges are conditioned on 3D module trajectories, the post-transition verification maneuver, and predicted docking observability; (2) a four-method ablation ladder through one simulation and autonomy stack, each rung differing only in the information its transition test consumes; and (3) a paired, layout-clustered protocol fixed before data, behind platform qualification gates and integrity rules that keep simulator truth out of autonomy and infrastructure failures out of robot outcomes.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Joint path and morphology.} Le et al.\ select among hTetro's four morphologies with a modified A*~\cite{le2018htetro}, and Cheng et al.\ plan coverage for reconfigurable tiling robots by graph search~\cite{cheng2019htetro} and a genetic algorithm~\cite{cheng2020htetro}. These establish joint search over pose and shape, for coverage, with shape changes actuated in place and no detached modules or docking.

\textbf{Simultaneous driving and reconfiguration.} Pankert et al.\ drive and reconfigure a steerable base simultaneously with MPC~\cite{pankert2022base}, and MIRRAX changes its wheel geometry for restricted spaces~\cite{cheah2023mirrax}. Both have fixed topology, and whether a change is feasible is a property of the controller, not of the site.

\textbf{Configuration selection in modular systems.} SMORES-EP maps task requirements to stored configurations and behaviors~\cite{jing2016smores}; Daudelin et al.\ select configurations from perceived environment properties~\cite{daudelin2018perception}, the closest architectural precedent; and FreeSN reconfigures autonomously with configuration matching and onboard sensing~\cite{tu2026freesn}. Detach--drive--redock and sensor-driven topology estimation are prior art. These systems condition where to reconfigure on symbolic regions or stored behaviors, not on geometric search over sites.

\textbf{Reconfiguration planning and control.} Reconfiguration planners generate module motions along a supplied trajectory~\cite{yoshida2002reconfig} or toward a specified target~\cite{liu2019distributed}; learned and QP controllers generalize across module graphs~\cite{whitman2023modular,liu2020qp}; freeform connectors relax where modules may attach~\cite{liang2020freebot}. In each, the target or the route is an input.

\textbf{Multi-modal and task-and-motion planning.} Search over $(x, y, \theta, m)$ is multi-modal planning, with modes joined only at transition configurations that must themselves be found~\cite{hauser2010multimodal}, and task-and-motion planning likewise interleaves discrete choices with geometric feasibility tests~\cite{garrett2021tamp}. We add no search machinery. We vary the transition test, from the two end configurations alone to the physical events that realize the switch and the sensing it relies on, and measure what each added condition is worth on executed missions, not planner completeness or speed.

\section{System}

\subsection{Platform and morphologies}
The robot is a sensor core carrying six self-mobile differential-drive pods, simulated in Gazebo under ROS~2 Jazzy and Nav2. Detached pods drive independently; assembled, one controller allocates a body twist to all wheels, which have bounded-effort actuation, vertical suspension, and anisotropic friction (rolling coefficient 1.2, lateral 0.08) whose axes are attached to the wheel, so rotation authority does not depend on world heading. \method{compact\_diff} is a skid-steer assembly with a 0.72\,m-wide safety footprint; \method{narrow\_tandem} is 1.76\,m long and 0.66\,m wide, so it alone passes a 0.70\,m doorway; the tallest is 0.30\,m. Other catalog morphologies are placeholders and excluded.

\subsection{Reconfiguration execution}
Each transition runs as a staged, sensor-driven procedure: detach, self-mobile relocation of each moved pod along its declared path, alignment from connector observations, latch, and verification of the observed topology, which is authoritative for safety. Assembled drive is inhibited throughout, and any partial or inconsistent topology moves execution to \texttt{RECOVERY\_REQUIRED}, left only by explicit reconciliation to a complete supported morphology. The navigator aligns the core to the planned site (0.03\,m, 0.05\,rad) or replans, abandons an in-place rotation that makes no progress, and trims yaw back to the planned heading after a commit. Connector capture and latches are idealized.

\subsection{Sensing}
Localization is AMCL on lidar and wheel odometry. Connector sensors at the core report pods within a 0.05--1.2\,m frustum, with covariance a function of range and no occlusion test; docking requires every moved pod visible with covariance trace at most 0.015. Their sight lines run along the pods' own relocation paths, so nothing outside the post-transition footprint can occlude them and location-dependent observability cannot arise onboard. We make it physical with an external \emph{fiducial station}: a pod is station-observed when the segment from the station is in range and unoccluded by the declared 3D environment, and otherwise carries a covariance trace of 0.04, above the gate. Occlusion comes from an \emph{elevated} screen whose underside is at 0.60\,m, above the robot and its sweeps, so it constrains neither driving nor geometric feasibility. Precision docking therefore depends on infrastructure, an assumption the results inherit; it is not a claim about self-contained connector sensing.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Hybrid state space and search}
The planner runs weighted A* over hybrid states $s = (x, y, \theta, m)$ on a 0.1\,m grid with discretized heading. Traversal edges use morphology-specific footprints and costs; a reconfiguration edge keeps the pose and changes $m$ through a catalog transition. Edge cost is
\begin{equation}
c = T + \lambda_E E + \lambda_R \bigl(-\log(1 - p)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with duration $T$, mechanical energy $E$, and failure probability $p$ (a declared per-transition prior; zero for traversal), plus a risk charge on unknown space. Traversal must be executable by the shared controllers, not only collision-free: heading-changing primitives keep 0.10\,m clearance, about the tracking and localization error observed in pilot records, while straight ones keep the exact check so the 0.70\,m doorway stays passable; every primitive keeps 0.03\,m from raised obstacles; and primitives move only forward, because no controller in the stack reverses. The planner logs a pre-action $p$ for each attempted transition so that calibration can be scored.

\subsection{Transition-feasibility edges}
A candidate site is tested in up to four stages; each failure leaves a structured per-module rejection reason in the trial record.

\textbf{(i) Planar admissibility.} The transition's planar clearance disk (radius 1.0\,m) must be free in the occupancy grid, and the target morphology's footprint must fit at the site.

\textbf{(ii) Module trajectories.} The relocation is expanded into sequential 3D pod, body, and wheel trajectories and sampled against the stationary core, placed pods, and the declared 3D obstacles (e.g., a raised shelf the 2D map cannot represent), with support and latch conditions at each target.

\textbf{(iii) Post-transition verification maneuver.} Before releasing drive, the robot yaws in place at $\pm 0.25$\,rad/s for 1.5\,s each way, then drives $\pm 0.12$\,m/s for 1.0\,s. The planner sweeps the rigid target body through the same commanded twists, read from the same catalog entry as the executor (a test binds the two), inflated by 0.07\,m for the site tolerance and part of the localization error, and rejects the edge on contact. A site must admit every motion performed there, not only the transformation.

\textbf{(iv) Docking observability.} From priors alone (the declared station pose, 3D environment, and transition geometry), a provider predicts whether each moved pod will be station-observed when docked. The edge is rejected if any is predicted not visible or above the 0.015 gate, the criteria the executor applies when docking. No evaluator truth enters.

\subsection{Methods as an ablation ladder}
All four methods share the search, cost model, mechanics, controllers, localization, maps, retry limits, and disturbance realizations; they differ only in the transition test (Table~\ref{tab:methods}).

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Layout families and neutral controls}
Three families target different rungs: \emph{reconfiguration workspace}, where raised 3D obstacles near the attractive staging area constrain module trajectories; \emph{docking observability}, where the station and screen shadow part of it; and \emph{combined constraints}. Every layout has one interior wall with a 0.70\,m doorway between a start room, where the robot must transform, and a goal room. Every fourth layout is a neutral control with walls and doorway only, in which all methods should agree.

\subsection{Layout population}
The layout is the sampling unit, so the estimand is only as broad as the generator. Two earlier generators were replaced before confirmatory data, the second after its pilot showed start rooms too short for the long body to transform and still turn onto the door line. The third, bound to the design hash, draws arenas of 7.1--8.0 by 3.2--4.0\,m, an interior wall at $x \in [3.5, 4.3]$\,m with the doorway anywhere leaving 0.7\,m of wall, start and goal off the door line so routes must turn, and five values each for the staging-band extent and half-height. A planner manipulation check must show separation in manipulated layouts and agreement in neutral ones before the design is frozen. On the 60 confirmatory layouts, checked before any trial, the treatment's site separates from \method{geometry\_coupled}'s in 41 of 45 manipulated layouts and from \method{feasibility\_coupled}'s in 26 of 30; every exception is a layout the treatment cannot plan, kept and scored against it, and all 15 neutral controls agree (Table~\ref{tab:manipulation}). \method{route\_first\_adaptation} plans in only 5 of the 60, neutral controls included, because its fixed shortest route reaches the doorway with no site that admits the transition; it is a weak rung in this population, and its failures do not measure the information it lacks.

\subsection{Paired seeds and schedule}
Each layout runs in three replicates under all four methods. World, sensing, friction, and fault seeds are independent deterministic streams shared by the four methods of a layout--replicate block; plant randomization draws ground friction in $[0.70, 1.10]$ and a spawn offset of $\pm 0.015$\,m and $\pm 0.035$\,rad, applied identically to Gazebo and the localization prior. Method order is randomized within each block, and pilot seeds are disjoint from the confirmatory design: 60 layouts, 20 per family, 720 trials (SHA-256 \texttt{c939f126\ldots{}5734}).

\subsection{Outcomes}
The primary outcome is completion within a 300\,s simulated deadline: reaching the goal with no collision, a valid observed topology in \texttt{READY}, and no unrecovered fault. The secondary outcome is deadline-penalized time, failures scored at 300\,s; successful-run time and mechanical work are reported separately so a completion/work trade-off is not hidden in one scalar.

Each terminal record carries one status, defined before pilot data. A \emph{planning failure} is a mission failure at 300\,s, never excluded or retried. A \emph{collision} is evaluator-inferred contact (clearance $\le 0$ between module poses and world geometry, debounced per module over 0.5\,s). \emph{Localization loss} is judged against evaluator truth: three consecutive 2\,Hz samples with planar error above 0.30\,m (normal AMCL error peaked at 0.126\,m over 124 qualification records), or a navigator failure naming localization or a transform. The earlier of first contact and first loss names any robot outcome, a completed mission counting only a loss episode still open at its end; infrastructure failures and planning failures are never re-attributed, and a \emph{timeout} is the deadline with no other status.
Secondary measures carry explicit denominators: minimum 3D clearance, localization RMSE against time-aligned truth, docking attempts, transition failures, planning latency, the failure taxonomy, the transition Brier score with reliability bins by method, and achieved site placement, the evaluator-truth distance of the core from each planned site when its transition begins, beside the nominal 0.03\,m target. Planner-decision diagnostics are manipulation checks and receive no tests.

\subsection{Analysis}
The primary estimand is the layout-balanced paired difference in completion probability: replicates are averaged within a layout and layouts weighted equally within the contrast's family scope (all three families; or docking observability and combined constraints). We report absolute differences with 95\% intervals from a bootstrap resampling whole layouts within family, and two-sided $p$ values from sign-flipping layout-level differences, exhaustive up to 20 layouts and otherwise a predeclared seeded Monte Carlo count reported with its standard error. Holm correction covers the two confirmatory $p$ values; family-level effects are heterogeneity diagnostics. An effect is claimed only when the adjusted test and the interval agree, and its magnitude is read against the smallest effect of interest by rules fixed before any confirmatory outcome was read.

Two sensitivity analyses are frozen before unblinding and replace nothing: a paired hierarchical logistic model per contrast with treatment and family fixed effects and a Gaussian layout random intercept, reported as marginal probability differences with a layout-cluster bootstrap of at least 400 refits; and a deadline-time analysis with a 300\,s point mass for failures.

\textbf{Smallest effect of interest and sample size.} The smallest effect of interest is 0.18 absolute completion probability for both contrasts, fixed before pilot data on operational grounds: the method adds feasibility and sensing models to every query and commits the robot to reconfigurations it would otherwise skip, which roughly one more completed mission in six must justify. The pilot supplies only nuisance parameters for a frozen grid (control rate, layout logit SD, paired-noise fraction, each with an adverse value), simulated for both scopes at $\alpha = 0.025$; the design passes when every cell's 95\% Wilson lower bound reaches 80\% power, and otherwise layouts are added, never the threshold changed. The 144-trial pilot found every layout all-or-nothing (neutral controls always completed, controls never did in manipulated layouts, replicates agreed), so the layout SD fit ran to 5 (adverse 10) and the paired-noise fraction was unidentified (taken as 0). Its first reading found no passing count and exposed a defect we disclose: the power model applied the effect as a fixed logit gap at the median layout, which at these spreads simulates an average effect of 0.04--0.08, not 0.18. With the gap solved so the layout-averaged effect is 0.18, and effect, grid, and threshold unchanged, the all-family scope passes at 36 layouts and the sensing scope needs 40 of its own, hence 60. The pilot's effect estimates never entered the grid.

\subsection{Platform qualification (Gate 0)}

\subsection{Plans executable as planned}
A comparison is only as fair as the execution layer the methods share: if a control fails because the controller executes its plan differently from how it was checked, the treatment is credited for an execution artifact. Each pilot failure was therefore traced against evaluator truth to the motion that diverged from the plan, and each fix was confirmed on reproduction trials copying the failing seeds, against acceptance criteria committed before the run, in an engineering design whose records cannot pass for study data. Five divergences were fixed: (i) the long body tracked turns loosely and swung into a shelf, so turns keep 0.10\,m; (ii) planned reverse steps were executed as unswept in-place turnarounds, so traversal is forward-only (largest narrow in-place turn 2.26--3.02\,rad before, at most 1.37\,rad after); (iii) a wheel allocation tuned before the friction repair turned the narrow body at $2.00\times$ command, now $1.17\times$; (iv) the controller aborted routes beside raised obstacles at zero margin, cleared by a finer obstacle raster with a 0.03\,m margin (32 trials beside the obstacle, no abort or contact); and (v) pure pursuit cut planned vertices so the long body met the 0.70\,m door off its line in 16 of 20 approaches, so the narrow body now servos each vertex (7 of 7 reproductions completed, nose within 0.036\,m of the door centreline). A shorter lookahead and a straight-approach planner rule were rejected on measurement, the latter because it made the treatment unplannable in a third of manipulated layouts where every control still planned. Each fix applies to all four methods through shared models or controllers and changes no method's information.

\subsection{Infrastructure failures and retries}
Robot failures are never retried or excluded. The runner retries a trial at most twice, only when the stack fails before the mission begins (e.g., a Nav2 lifecycle timeout); each attempt keeps an immutable log and an fsync'd audit entry embedded in the terminal record. A wedged observer process or a DDS transport failure is classified as infrastructure by a parent watchdog, never as a robot outcome. One harness defect is disclosed with its fix: under host load the ROS clock occasionally stopped at mission start while Gazebo kept stepping (4 of 78 qualification trials), because the bridge's discovery dropped the simulator's publishers; loopback discovery removed it under the same load (0 of 14 against 3 of 13), and any recurrence is detected as a clock discontinuity. In the pilot, 3 of 144 trials needed one retry and none ended as an infrastructure failure. \withresults{In the confirmatory study, \resInfraAttempts{} of \resLaunchAttempts{} launch attempts were lost to infrastructure before a mission began, \resTrialsRetried{} trials were retried, and \resInfraFailures{} ended as an infrastructure failure.}{\pending{confirmatory infrastructure-attempt rates.}}

\subsection{Timing, truth isolation, and provenance}
\textbf{Timing.} Every window that paces robot motion advances on the simulated clock, the wall clock only a stall backstop, and validation rejects any stage that ran less simulated time than it commanded; a wall-clock window would make travel scale with host load and pass a measurement artifact off as a mechanical fault.

\textbf{Ground-truth isolation.} Clearance, contact, localization error, and site placement come from evaluator streams; no truth stream feeds planning, control, recovery, or readiness, which a static test enforces over the autonomy packages. A telemetry audit rejects records with dropped diagnostics, missing or unsynchronized truth, or stored errors that disagree with recomputation.

\textbf{Provenance and retention.} Analysis refuses incomplete blocks, changed specifications, unpaired seeds, and mixed commit, configuration, or design hashes. Raw records are retained as compressed archives beside committed audits with per-record and campaign digests, so every figure can be recomputed from the records it came from.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Simulation only.} All results are from Gazebo. Connector capture and latches are idealized, contact is geometric (inferred from poses against declared geometry within 1.0\,m of a known obstacle, not from a physics contact sensor), and the feasibility checks are geometric validation, not physical certification.

\textbf{Two morphologies.} Only \method{compact\_diff} and \method{narrow\_tandem} are in scope, connected by one transition pair. Larger morphology sets may change how often site choice binds.

\textbf{Docking observability depends on infrastructure.} The sensing contrast is realized through an external fiducial station and an elevated screen. The planner's prediction and the station's measurement share one geometric rule with different inputs, so an audit of one against the other checks placement and inputs, not the rule.

\textbf{Site tolerance is nominal.} The 0.03\,m site tolerance is a control target servoed on an estimate whose error has reached 0.10\,m, so it is not an achieved-placement bound. The 0.07\,m sweep margin covers the tolerance plus 0.04\,m of estimation error; error beyond that is unmodelled, and a linear box inflation is an imperfect model of angular error. We therefore report measured placement beside the nominal target. The margin cannot grow without enlarging the clearance disk, which changes site selection.

\textbf{Scope of the 3D model.} Declared 3D obstacles enter traversal only as driving obstacles; ordinary traversal edges are otherwise checked against the 2D occupancy grid. The transition risk prior is a declared per-transition constant; a learned or sensing-dependent risk model is future work.

\textbf{Generalization.} The population is generated rooms with one wall and one doorway. Conclusions extend to that population, not to arbitrary environments.
